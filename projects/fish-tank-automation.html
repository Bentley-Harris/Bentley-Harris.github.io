Fish Tank Automation System

2026 · Electronics & Embedded
  • Arduino
  • MOSFET switching
  • RTC
  • 3D printing

An automation system for my beloved fish tank, currently in its final stages. It runs the tank light on a schedule and feeds the fish automatically, both driven by an Arduino Nano with a menu on a small OLED display. I built it because the tank needed a routine that keeps running when I go home during uni breaks.

Key details

  • Reused the tank's existing light instead of replacing it: the Arduino switches it on and off on a schedule through MOSFETs that hijack the original light circuitry.
  • 3D printed an automatic fish feeder. It is not my original design, but I had to modify it to stop it jamming, and it has now run reliably for more than thirty consecutive days. I chose a screw-based design over a trapdoor or push-based one on purpose, by thinking through the worst case: if a screw feeder fails, the food stays trapped inside, but a trapdoor stuck open could overfeed the fish and wipe out the tank.
  • 0.96-inch OLED display and a real-time clock module, with a scrollable menu and several operating modes.
  • Built and tested an earlier variant with RF remote control. It worked and I have footage of it, but I cut it from the final design to focus on reliability and consistency rather than flashy features.
  • Still on a breadboard. When I get time over summer, I plan to finish designing a custom through-hole (THT) PCB and build the circuit into a compact, professional enclosure, so it is a finished package I can be proud of. I also plan to swap the Arduino Nano for an ESP32-C3, for its smaller package and onboard Wi-Fi, which opens up the option of connecting the system to my phone.

What I learned

Reliability matters far more than features on anything that runs unattended. The feeder has kept going for over a month, and that is mostly because the mechanism is simple. Earlier versions had more moving parts that found more ways to jam, and more flaws in the code that could have led to continuous overfeeding.

A big part of the project was thinking through the ways things could go wrong. What if there is a power outage and the tank misses a feed? What if the system is packed away and misses a few feeds? What if a glitch or a failed component gives it bad information? Questions like these are why I added several safety measures, including a strict hard-coded limit of two feeds in 24 hours and one feed per hour, so there can never be accidental back-to-back feeds.

Removing the RF remote was the right call even though it worked. Building something and then cutting it is not wasted time. I would rather have tested the idea than wondered about it, and getting the RF link working and troubleshooting it taught me a lot that I plan to use on future builds.
