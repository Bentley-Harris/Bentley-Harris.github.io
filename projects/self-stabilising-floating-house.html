Self-Stabilising Floating House

2026 · Electronics & Embedded
  • Arduino
  • PID control
  • KiCad
  • CAD
  • Telemetry

This was a university team project and the largest thing I have worked on. It is a house on a floating base that keeps itself level when the water underneath it moves, sensing the tilt and correcting it rather than riding with it. I was the team leader, and as well as designing and building all of the electronics and controls, I did the CAD for every iteration of the 3D printed structure and winch system. I put more than fifty hours of design and build work into it myself, and the write-up below covers the parts I worked on.

The brief was station-keeping flood-resistant housing: a structure that stays upright and holds its position while the water beneath it moves and fans drive wind across it at roughly 2 m/s. It had to fit a 300 × 250 × 280 mm envelope with the base no more than 80 mm deep, keep a 155 × 155 × 150 mm open internal volume, float with an extra 1 kg shifting test load aboard, and run entirely from the 12 V, 2 A supply provided at the test rig. Scoring came from a camera tracking system that measured how far the structure drifted and rotated over a five-minute run, along with readings from onboard sensors in the control box.

Key details

  • Buoyancy from layers of XPS foam sheet bonded together with epoxy, with the seams reinforced with water-resistant tape, 300 × 250 × 70 mm. I cut the centre out to press-fit the printed chassis and secured it with more epoxy, all done in my dorm room. Foam over a sealed hull was deliberate: a breached hull sinks, whereas breached foam keeps floating.
  • Superstructure designed by me in Fusion 360 over three iterations, then 3D printed in PET-G, press-fitted into the foam and sealed with epoxy and silicone. I made the call to switch from the original corflute exterior to a printed structure, because the extra rigidity improved the accuracy of the IMU readings and kept the IMU parallel to the base of the house. The IMU mounts to the roof, so any flex in that surface feeds straight into the tilt readings.
  • All of the electronics sit on the roof, so a breach in the hull would not put the circuitry in the water. The shifting weight sits as low in the structure as possible.
  • Four N20 12 V 100 RPM gearmotors at the corners, each on a printed two-piece clamp mount that I designed and took through three iterations, driving a printed ~15 mm spool locked to the D-shaft with a grub screw.
  • Tethers of 50 lb braided nylon run from each spool through PTFE pass-throughs to two barrel-swivel anchors on the base plate, rigged as V-pairs because there were only two attachment points. Lead sinkers hold a baseline pre-tension so the lines never go slack between corrections, which could cause backlash and tangle the line.
  • Control electronics on hand-soldered stripboard, laid out in KiCad and built as a two-board sandwich stack on M3 standoffs and a custom threaded-rod pole rather than a breadboard, for vibration resistance, density and, honestly, a bit of style.
  • Power split into a 12 V motor rail and a logic rail: a buck converter drops 12 V to 7.5 V, then a 1N5819 Schottky diode brings it to about 7.2 V at the Arduino Nano's VIN, with the on-board regulator producing 5 V. A 660 µF bulk capacitor absorbs the current transients from motors reversing, and 0.1 µF ceramics across each motor keep commutation noise off the IMU's I²C readings.
  • Tilt sensed by an Adafruit LSM6DS3TR-C 6-DoF IMU over I²C, with accelerometer and gyroscope fused through a complementary filter at 50 Hz.
  • Two TB6612FNG dual H-bridges driving the four motors. I chose them over the older L298N because the lower on-resistance leaves more of the supply voltage at the motor, and they are smaller and more efficient overall. I also fitted small heatsinks to them. They did not really need them, but it was a cheap extra precaution.
  • Firmware on an Arduino Nano running a roll-axis PID loop with anti-windup and integral leak, a four-channel motor mixing matrix, and a five-state machine covering halt, run, manual, IMU fault and an emergency float mode that reels out automatically if tilt exceeds 10° for a sustained period.
  • Wired operator pod with a CAD-designed printed enclosure and its own soldered board: one pot scales maximum motor authority and doubles as a kill input, a second sets the PID deadband between 0.5° and 5°, and a centre-return rocker reels all four tethers in or out. The rocker is there because the sensors can read the tilt but not the changing depth of the water, so depth adjustment relied on the operator. Two indicator LEDs show the 12 V and 5 V rails separately, so a dead logic rail is obvious at a glance.
  • Logged every IMU reading from every test run into large CSV files, producing spreadsheets thousands of lines long. I used AI tools to help analyse those datasets and optimise the tuning, finding where the loop was overshooting instead of guessing from watching it move.

What I learned

The control loop was the hard part, and guessing at PID values got me nowhere. What worked was logging every IMU reading and basing the tuning on real trends in the data. Looking at the logs after each run is what actually moved it forward, because I could see exactly where and by how much the system was overshooting instead of guessing from watching it move.

The decision that saved the most time was putting live parameter adjustment on the handheld controller. Changing sensitivity mid-test instead of reflashing the Arduino after every attempt turned a slow cycle into a fast one, and I would build that in from the start on anything with parameters to tune.

This project was also a big lesson for me in a different way. I was so invested in the idea of building a complex PID system that I took on something that was probably well outside the intended scope of the project. I wanted to do it to learn, for the challenge, and because I was convinced it would be the best approach. In reality, that choice cost me a lot of time and a lot of headaches, for results that a practised human operator could probably have matched. Perhaps an elegant, complex solution to an engineering problem is not as clever as it looks, and the cleverer option is the one that makes the best use of time and resources, even if it is not revolutionary. If it works, it works.

Designing a fully autonomous PID control system from scratch, with no prior PID experience, on a time and budget deadline, with cheap components, in my first semester of university, was probably a bit unwise. I would still do it all again, because the knowledge and experience I gained were probably the most valuable thing I took from my first year. Sometimes the best thing you can do in engineering is step back and look at the whole situation, instead of blindly pushing on because you really want something to work. Machines do not work well because of their creator's willpower, desire or dreams.
