Self-Stabilising Floating House

2026 · Electronics & Embedded
  • Arduino
  • PID control
  • KiCad
  • CAD
  • Telemetry

This was a university team project and the largest thing I have worked on. It is a house on a floating base that keeps itself level when the water underneath it moves, sensing the tilt and correcting it rather than riding with it. It took more than fifty hours of design work across the team, and the write-up below covers the electronics, firmware and operator controls I contributed to.

The brief was station-keeping flood-resistant housing: a structure that stays upright and holds its position while the water beneath it moves and fans drive wind across it at roughly 2 m/s. It had to fit a 300 × 250 × 280 mm envelope with the base no more than 80 mm deep, keep a 155 × 155 × 150 mm open internal volume, float with an extra 3 kg test load aboard, and run entirely from the 12 V, 2 A supply provided at the test rig. Scoring came from a camera tracking system that measured how far the structure drifted and rotated over a five-minute run.

Key details

  • Buoyancy from a closed-cell XPS foam billet, 250 × 250 × 70 mm, with the centre machined out to press-fit the printed chassis. Foam over a sealed hull was deliberate: a breached hull sinks, whereas breached foam keeps floating.
  • Superstructure designed in CAD and 3D printed in PET-G, press-fitted into the foam and sealed with epoxy and silicone. PET-G over PLA for layer adhesion and heat resistance, and because the IMU mounts to the roof, so any flex in that surface feeds straight into the tilt readings.
  • Four N20 12 V 100 RPM gearmotors at the corners, each on a printed two-piece clamp mount, driving a printed ~15 mm spool locked to the D-shaft with a grub screw.
  • Tethers of 50 lb braided nylon run from each spool through PTFE pass-throughs to two barrel-swivel anchors on the base plate, rigged as V-pairs. Lead sinkers hold a baseline pre-tension so the lines never go slack between corrections.
  • Control electronics on hand-soldered stripboard, laid out in KiCad and built as a two-board sandwich stack on M3 standoffs rather than a breadboard, for vibration resistance and density.
  • Power split into a 12 V motor rail and a logic rail: a buck converter drops 12 V to 7.5 V, then a 1N5819 Schottky diode brings it to about 7.2 V at the Arduino Nano's VIN, with the on-board regulator producing 5 V. A 660 µF bulk capacitor absorbs the current transients from motors reversing, and 0.1 µF ceramics across each motor keep commutation noise off the IMU's I²C lines.
  • Tilt sensed by an Adafruit LSM6DS3TR-C 6-DoF IMU over I²C, with accelerometer and gyroscope fused through a complementary filter at 50 Hz.
  • Two TB6612FNG dual H-bridges driving the four motors, chosen over the older L298N because the lower on-resistance leaves more of the supply voltage at the motor.
  • Firmware on an Arduino Nano running a roll-axis PID loop with anti-windup and integral leak, a four-channel motor mixing matrix, and a five-state machine covering halt, run, manual, IMU fault and an emergency float mode that reels out automatically if tilt exceeds 10° for a sustained period.
  • Wired operator pod with a CAD-designed printed enclosure and its own soldered board: one pot scales maximum motor authority and doubles as a kill input, a second sets the PID deadband between 0.5° and 5°, and a centre-return rocker reels all four tethers in or out. Two indicator LEDs show the 12 V and 5 V rails separately, so a dead logic rail is obvious at a glance.
  • Logged every IMU reading from every test run, producing spreadsheets thousands of lines long, and analysed those datasets to find where the loop was overshooting rather than guessing from watching it move.

What I learned

The control loop was the hard part, and guessing at PID values got me nowhere. Logging every IMU reading and looking at the data afterwards is what actually moved it forward, because I could see exactly where and by how much the system was overshooting instead of guessing from watching it move.

The decision that saved the most time was putting live parameter adjustment on the handheld controller. Changing sensitivity mid-test instead of reflashing the Arduino after every attempt turned a slow cycle into a fast one, and I would build that in from the start on anything with parameters to tune.
